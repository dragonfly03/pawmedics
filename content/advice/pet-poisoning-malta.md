Most poisonings happen at home, with products we use every day. Many of them are easy to prevent once you know what to look for. This guide covers the poisons we see most often in dogs and cats, and what to do if your pet eats or touches one.

**If you think your pet has been poisoned, call us now.** Don't wait for signs to appear.

## Rat and mouse bait

Rat and mouse poisons are widely used in Malta, in homes, gardens, fields and around bins. The most common types stop the blood from clotting. A dog or cat that eats bait, or eats a poisoned rodent, may look completely normal at first. Signs of bleeding usually appear **three to seven days later**: weakness, pale gums, breathing difficulty, nosebleeds, bruising, blood in the urine or stools, or a swollen belly.

This delay is what makes rat bait so dangerous. If you know or suspect your pet has eaten bait, call us straight away. If we see your pet soon after, we may be able to make it vomit the bait up, and there is an effective antidote, vitamin K1, which works best when given before bleeding starts.

**To prevent it:** use bait only in tamper-proof boxes, place them where pets can't reach, and tell your neighbours if you have a dog that roams the garden. Ask anyone treating your home or farm for pests to tell you where they've put bait.

## Slug and snail pellets

Snail pellets are attractive to dogs and can be very toxic. Depending on the ingredient, signs can start within an hour: twitching, wobbliness, drooling, panting, a high temperature and seizures. This is an emergency. Keep pellets locked away and choose pet-friendly ways of protecting your plants.

## Human medicines

Never give your pet a medicine meant for people unless a vet has told you to.

- **Paracetamol** is very dangerous for cats. A single tablet can be fatal. It also harms dogs at higher doses.
- **Ibuprofen, aspirin and similar anti-inflammatories** can cause stomach ulcers and kidney damage in dogs and cats.
- **Other medicines** such as antidepressants, ADHD tablets, heart and blood pressure pills, and cannabis products can all poison pets.

Dropped pills disappear quickly into a dog's mouth. Keep medicines in closed cupboards, never in handbags on the floor, and pick up anything dropped straight away.

## Flea, tick and sandfly treatments

Many products for dogs contain **permethrin** or similar ingredients that are safe for dogs but can kill cats. Signs in cats include twitching, tremors, drooling and seizures.

- Never use a dog product on a cat, even a small amount.
- After applying a spot-on to your dog, keep it apart from your cats until the treatment has dried.
- Keep dog collars that repel sandflies away from cats that like to groom or sleep against your dog.

Always read the label, and ask us which products are safe for a home with both dogs and cats.

## Plants and flowers

**Lilies** cause kidney failure in cats. Every part of the plant is toxic, including the pollen and the water in the vase. **Oleander**, common in Maltese gardens and roadsides, is toxic to dogs and cats and affects the heart. Sago palms, often grown as ornamental plants, are toxic to dogs. If you're unsure about a plant, send us a photo.

## Foods

Chocolate, grapes and raisins, xylitol (a sweetener in sugar-free gum and some peanut butters), onions and garlic, and macadamia nuts are the foods we see causing problems most often. Our [Christmas safety guide](/advice/christmas-pet-safety/) explains each one.

## Garden and car chemicals

- **Antifreeze and screenwash** containing ethylene glycol taste sweet and can cause kidney failure. Wipe up spills and store containers closed.
- **Weedkillers, insecticides and fertilisers** can be harmful. Keep pets off treated areas until they're dry, or for as long as the label says.
- **Cleaning products** such as bleach, dishwasher tablets and drain cleaner can burn the mouth and stomach.

## If you think your pet has been poisoned

1. **Move your pet away** from the poison and stop it eating any more.
2. **Call us straight away**, or the emergency helpline if we're closed. Tell us what your pet ate, roughly how much, when, and your pet's approximate weight.
3. **Bring the packaging**, a photo of the label, or a sample of the plant.
4. **Don't make your pet sick at home** unless a vet tells you to. Salt water, hydrogen peroxide and other home remedies can be dangerous.
5. **If poison is on the skin or fur**, stop your pet licking it and ask us whether to wash it off.

## Common questions

### My dog ate rat poison but seems fine. Do I still need to call?

Yes. Many rat poisons take three to seven days to cause bleeding, and by then your dog can be seriously ill. Treatment works best when it starts early.

### Can I give my dog paracetamol or ibuprofen for pain?

No. Ibuprofen can cause stomach ulcers and kidney damage in dogs, and paracetamol is dangerous at the wrong dose. Paracetamol is extremely dangerous for cats. Call us and we'll prescribe pain relief that's safe for your pet.

### I put my dog's flea treatment on my cat. What should I do?

Call us immediately, even if your cat seems well. If the product contains permethrin, your cat may need treatment to prevent seizures. Bring the packet with you.

### Should I make my pet vomit if it eats something poisonous?

Not at home. Some poisons do more damage coming back up, and home methods such as salt water can be dangerous. A vet can decide whether vomiting is safe and give a medicine that works quickly.
